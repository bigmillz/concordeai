## q30 — Quantization explained

**Q:** Explain what 4-bit quantization does to an LLM and when it actually matters. Keep it short and technical.

*24.43s, 1873 chars, models: Gemma 4 26B, searched: False, drafts: *

Quantization maps high-precision weights (usually **FP16** or **BF16**) to a smaller set of discrete integer values, effectively compressing the model's memory footprint so it can fit on consumer hardware.

In a standard 16-bit model, each weight uses 16 bits; moving to 4-bit reduces the memory requirement for weights by **75%**. The accuracy loss occurs because you are forcing a continuous range of values into just 16 possible "buckets" ($2^4$).

To mitigate this, modern techniques like **GPTQ**, **AWQ**, or **GGUF** use block-wise scaling. They group weights into blocks and apply a specific scale and zero-point to each group, ensuring the distribution of the original weights is approximated as closely as possible within the 4-bit constraints.

## When It Matters

**VRAM Constraints** — This is the primary driver. A 70B parameter model requires roughly **140GB** of VRAM at FP16. At 4-bit, that requirement drops to approximately **35-40GB**, allowing it to run on a single Mac Studio or a dual-GPU workstation.

**Inference Latency** — Because the weights are smaller, they require less bandwidth to move from VRAM to the compute cores. Since many LLM workloads are **memory-bandwidth bound** rather than compute-bound, moving 4-bit weights often results in significant increases in tokens-per-second.

**The Scaling Law Trade-off** — The loss in intelligence is relative to the model's original size. A 70B model at 4-bit will almost always outperform a 7B model at FP16 in reasoning tasks, despite the precision loss. However, the "quantization error" becomes much more destructive on very small models (e.g., sub-7B), where the intelligence loss is highly noticeable.

The sweet spot for most deployments is **4-bit to 6-bit**. If you drop below 4-bit (to 3-bit or 2-bit), the model's ability to follow instructions or maintain coherence tends to collapse.
