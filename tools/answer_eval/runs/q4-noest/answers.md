## q30 — Quantization explained

**Q:** Explain what 4-bit quantization does to an LLM and when it actually matters. Keep it short and technical.

*24.25s, 2058 chars, models: Gemma 4 26B, searched: False, drafts: *

4-bit quantization maps the high-precision weights of a model (typically **FP16** or **BF16**) onto a much smaller, 4-bit integer grid, reducing the memory footprint by roughly **4x**.

In a standard 16-bit setup, each parameter occupies 2 bytes. Moving to 4-bit reduces this to roughly **0.5 bytes** per parameter (plus a small overhead for scaling factors and metadata). This allows a 70B parameter model, which would normally require ~140GB of VRAM, to fit into roughly **40GB** of VRAM.

The trade-off is **quantization error**: rounding the original weights to the nearest representable 4-bit value introduces noise, which increases **perplexity** (the model's uncertainty). 

Modern quantization-aware techniques like **GPTQ** or **GGUF** (used in `llama.cpp`) mitigate this by using calibration datasets to minimize the error during the mapping process.

It matters most when deciding between model size and precision:

*   **Scaling vs. Precision:** A larger model at 4-bit often outperforms a much smaller model at 16-bit. For example, a **70B model at 4-bit** will generally be more capable than a **7B model at FP16**, even with the precision loss.
*   **Small Models (<10B):** Precision loss is much more visible here. Because these models have less "parameter redundancy," 4-bit quantization can lead to significant degradation in logic and coherence.
*   **Large Models (>70B):** These models are much more resilient to quantization. The "intelligence" lost to rounding is often negligible compared to the benefit of running a massive model on consumer-grade hardware.
*   **Reasoning-heavy tasks:** In complex tasks like mathematical proofs or strict code generation, the "quantization tax" might manifest as increased hallucinations or logical lapses.

**The verdict:** 4-bit is the best option when your goal is to maximize parameter count within a fixed VRAM budget. If you have the headroom, 8-bit or 16-bit is mathematically superior, but for most production and local inference, 4-bit is the optimal balance of efficiency and capability.
